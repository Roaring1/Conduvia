    Field notes¶

    The real process behind these items — what they are, how they were used, and why they matter. Tap to expand.

    A-frame gin pole

    Temporary A-frame crane with a pulley for lifting heavy loads.

    A lashed A-frame with a pulley or block hung at the apex, guyed by ropes and used as a simple hand-powered crane for raising loads.

    Real-world use · Crews assemble a-frame gin poles from on-site timbers, lash them, rig a block and tackle, and then hoist stones, logs, or machinery where full cranes cannot reach or are not economical.

    • When · Ancient through modern construction; used in classical building, medieval works, and early industrial sites, and still used in remote or temporary jobs.
    • Where · Building sites, dockyards, logging camps, tower erection, and any place lifting capacity is needed without a permanent crane.

    Why it matters · An A-frame gin pole is the simplest crane you can build from local wood and rope. The frame carries compression, the guys manage tipping, and the pulley and rope translate human pull into vertical lift. In Conduvia it’s the entry point for believable heavy lifting before iron cranes and powered hoists arrive.

    coke fuel

    Hard, porous lumps of almost pure carbon made by baking bituminous coal without air; the standard fuel and structural charge in modern blast furnaces.

    Real-world use · Charged along with iron ore and flux into the blast furnace, coke acts as both high-temperature fuel and permeable scaffold that lets hot air and gases flow through the burden while supporting its weight.

    • When · From the 18th century onward in Britain and then worldwide, coke replaced charcoal as the dominant fuel for large-scale ironmaking.
    • Where · Found in ore yards and stockpiles beside blast furnaces, in coke plant rail cars, and scattered as black, honeycombed lumps around ironworks.

    Why it matters · Coke is what you get after driving almost everything but carbon out of good coking coal. In a blast furnace burden, coke does three jobs at once: • It burns in the hot blast to generate very high temperatures. • It reacts chemically to strip oxygen from the iron ore. • Its strong, porous structure keeps the column of ore and flux from collapsing, leaving pathways for gases to move. Design-wise, this is your “Tier-up fuel” that justifies tall furnaces and more extreme metal flowsheets.

    steel ingot

    A solid block of cast steel from a converter or open-hearth heat, intended to be reheated and rolled or forged into plates, beams, rails, and other shapes.

    Real-world use · Molten steel is poured into ingot molds in a casting pit; once solidified and stripped, ingots are reheated in soaking pits and run through rolling mills to become usable product.

    • When · Dominant steel casting form from the late 19th into the mid-20th century, before continuous casting replaced most ingot-based practice.
    • Where · Casting pits and soaking pits in integrated mills, with ranks of molds and overhead cranes moving glowing ingots to the rolling line.

    Why it matters · Steel ingots are the “frozen state” of bulk steelmaking. Rather than casting directly into final shapes, historic mills almost always cast into ingots first, then reheated and rolled them. This gave flexibility: the same heat could become plate, rails, beams, or shafting depending on which mill it visited. In your progression, ingots are the handleable step between liquid steel in a furnace and the many specialized stock forms your later machinery and buildings demand.
