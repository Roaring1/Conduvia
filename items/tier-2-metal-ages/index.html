    Field notes¶

    The real process behind these items — what they are, how they were used, and why they matter. Tap to expand.

    bronze ingot

    Cast lump of copper–tin alloy ready for remelting, forging, or trade.

    Real-world use · Smelters cast bronze into convenient shapes--cakes, bars, or ‘oxhide’ slabs--that can be shipped, reheated, and cast or hammered into tools, weapons, and fittings.

    • When · Characteristic of Bronze Age metallurgy and later bronze-working wherever the alloy remains in use.
    • Where · Stored near casting hearths, in merchant inventories, and in hoards ready to be recast into whatever is needed.

    Why it matters · Bronze ingots represent metal as a circulating currency of work: easily re-melted, re-cast, or cut up as needed. They also show that alloying often happens upstream of the final toolmaker. A village smith might never smelt copper or tin, but they will understand exactly how to work a bar of sound bronze.

    burned lime (quicklime)

    Calcium oxide made by burning limestone hot enough to drive off carbon dioxide; an aggressive, caustic powder used as flux, binder, and chemical reagent.

    Real-world use · Charged into iron and steel furnaces as a flux, quicklime reacts with silica, phosphorus, and other impurities to form a liquid slag that can be tapped off. It also underpins mortar, plaster, agriculture, and water treatment.

    • When · Documented since antiquity for building mortars; in iron and steelmaking, lime flux became increasingly central from the 18th century onward and remains essential today.
    • Where · Produced in lime kilns near quarries and ironworks, then stored in covered sheds or barrels to keep it from reacting with moisture before use.

    Why it matters · Quicklime is what limestone becomes after a very hot cook. In ironmaking and steelmaking, it’s the “garbage collector” that rides along with molten metal. It grabs unwanted elements, especially silica and phosphorus, and locks them into slag. Historically, basic-lined converters and basic open-hearth furnaces only worked because their linings and slags carried enough lime to pull phosphorus out of high-P ores and pig iron. In play, this is your aggressive flux that unlocks higher-grade metal routes.

    clay bloomery furnace

    Tall clay shaft furnace fired with charcoal and air blast to smelt iron ore into a spongy bloom rather than liquid metal.

    Real-world use · Ore and charcoal are charged into the shaft while bellows blow through tuyeres near the base. At bloomery temperatures the ore is reduced and partially melts, forming a solid, slaggy iron bloom that is later hammered to consolidate.

    • When · Used from the advent of iron metallurgy (late 2nd to early 1st millennium BCE in many regions) through the medieval period, before high hot-blast furnaces.
    • Where · Early ironworking sites: hilltop furnaces, woodland ironworks, and small rural smelting operations near ore and charcoal supplies.

    Why it matters · The bloomery is the first true iron smelter. It does not pour molten iron; instead it yields a glowing sponge of iron and slag that must be forged. Its clay shell and charcoal charge tie it directly to the pottery and charcoal chains. In a teaching context it demonstrates how ceramics, fuel, and air-handling all have to be solved before a society can move from native metals to smelted iron.

    iron anvil block

    Solid block of iron or steel used as a striking surface for forging blooms, bars, and tools.

    Real-world use · Early anvils range from repurposed iron blooms and simple blocks to more refined welded constructions. They provide mass and rebound so forging blows move the hot workpiece instead of the support.

    • When · Iron anvils appear alongside bloomery iron; specialized forged anvils become more common through the medieval and early modern periods.
    • Where · In smithies, bloom-refining forges, and later dedicated toolmaking shops.

    Why it matters · Without a good anvil, bloom refining is punishingly inefficient. The anvil block is therefore a key “capital good”: a heavy, durable object that makes every later forging task easier. It also marks a shift from working stone on stone to working hot iron on iron--subtly changing what shapes and tolerances are practical.

    iron bloom

    Spongy, slag-filled lump of wrought iron straight from a bloomery furnace--hot, rough, and not yet fully consolidated.

    Real-world use · Smelters drag the bloom from the furnace, then smiths repeatedly hammer, fold, and cut it to squeeze out slag and weld the metallic portion into usable bars.

    • When · The standard output of bloomery ironmaking from its beginnings through the late medieval period wherever blast furnaces had not yet taken over.
    • Where · At the base of bloomery shafts, on forging blocks near the furnace, and in primary smithies close to ore and charcoal supplies.

    Why it matters · The bloom is neither ore nor finished metal; it is a transitional object that demands heavy forging before it behaves like bar iron. Showing blooms as explicit items lets you emphasize how much labor stood between “first smelt” and real tools--and why early iron was precious even when ore was common.

    pig iron ingot

    High-carbon iron--typically 3–4% carbon--cast from a blast furnace into ingots that are too brittle for direct structural use but perfect as feed for refining into wrought iron or steel.

    Real-world use · Drawn off from the blast furnace hearth and cast into runners and ‘pigs’ on sand beds or molds, pig iron is then remelted in finery, puddling, Bessemer, or open-hearth furnaces to become lower-carbon wrought iron or steel.

    • When · Produced wherever blast furnaces operated from the early modern era onward; volumes exploded during the 19th-century steel boom.
    • Where · Pig beds, casting houses, and stockyards near furnace plants, with characteristic short ingots stacked in heaps or loaded into rail cars.

    Why it matters · Pig iron is the “raw output” of blast furnaces: not yet good structural metal, but rich in energy and carbon for the next refining step. Historically, the shape and logistics of pig casting mattered--how fast you could cast, break, and move pigs limited how hard you could drive your blast furnace. In your flowsheet, pig iron sits between ore and finished steels, giving you branching options: send it into finery/puddling style routes or into converters and open-hearth shops.

    tailings

    Residual sand and mud left after valuable minerals have been removed from crushed ore.

    Real-world use · In real mines, tailings accumulate in piles, ponds, or dams. They may still hold small amounts of metal, but not enough to justify further treatment with the available technology--unless prices or processes change later.

    • When · A constant companion of ore dressing from ancient washing tables to modern flotation plants.
    • Where · Downstream of sluices, washers, and concentrating gear--often in low ground or engineered ponds to keep solids from choking rivers and channels.

    Why it matters · Tailings embody the idea that every process has leftovers. They are the physical record of all the material that did not make the economic cut. In Conduvia terms they can be both a nuisance (space, contamination) and a future opportunity (re-treatment with better tech). Showing them explicitly communicates that mining is as much about what you throw away as what you keep.

    wrought iron bar

    Refined bar of low-carbon wrought iron, fibrous with slag but tough and forgeable.

    Real-world use · Bars rolled or hammered from blooms form the basic stock for nails, straps, tools, and structural members. Wrought iron’s slag stringers make it resistant to fatigue and corrosion for many uses.

    • When · Dominant bar stock from the bloomery and early blast-furnace eras up to the adoption of mild steel in the 19th century.
    • Where · On racks at smithies, in bridge and building frames, as tie rods, straps, and fittings wherever iron construction is affordable.

    Why it matters · Wrought iron bar is the “plank” of the iron age: a standard stock form that gets cut, punched, and bent into almost anything. Treating it as a distinct item from raw bloom underscores all the invisible refining work that has already happened before a player forges their first recognizable tool.
